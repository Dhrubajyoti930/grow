The Tacoma Narrows

November 7, 1940. A day etched into engineering history. The bridge, known as "Galloping Gertie," didn't fall because of a marching army or perfect synchronicity.

For decades, the collapse was mythologized as a case of resonance—the bridge being pushed to its natural frequency by the wind. But this is a ghost story for physicists.

The true culprit was aeroelastic flutter. As the wind pushed against the stiff, plate-girder design of the deck, it created swirling vortices. These vortices acted as a feedback loop, feeding energy into the bridge’s own motion faster than the structure could dissipate it.

It was a self-excited oscillation. The bridge wasn't reacting to a rhythm; it was actively harvesting energy from the wind to destroy itself. By 11:00 AM, the torsion reached a breaking point, and the steel gave way to the air.
